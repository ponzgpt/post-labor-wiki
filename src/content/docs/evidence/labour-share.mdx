<Aside type="tip" title="In short">
If workers matter less to production, their slice of income should shrink. In the US it has: the official labour share of business income is lower now than at any time before 2000. But the long-run picture is a hump, not a cliff, and part of the story is benefits replacing wages.
</Aside>

## The official measure

<Tag kind="empirical" />The Bureau of Labor Statistics tracks how much of nonfarm business output is paid to workers, including an estimate for the self-employed. From 1947 to 2000 the index never fell below {f1(pre2000Low)}. In {last(ls).x} it averaged {f1(last(ls).v)} (2017 = 100).

## Not just an American story

<Tag kind="empirical" />Across countries, the labour share has declined significantly since the early 1980s; the authors attribute roughly half of the fall to the falling relative price of investment goods, which made it cheaper to replace labour with capital <Cite id="karabarbounis2014" at="abstract" />. The ILO's modelled estimates, which include the self-employed, put the world's labour income share at {f1(world[0].v)}% of GDP in {world[0].x} and {f1(last(world).v)}% in {last(world).x}.

## Why it matters for post-labor

<Tag kind="analytical" />Post-Labor Economics reads this decline as an early phase of the Great Decoupling: output keeps growing while the share paid for human work shrinks <Cite id="shapiro2025six" at="part II" />. If that continues, more income flows to whoever owns the machines, which is why it focuses on ownership rather than wages.

<Skeptics>
- **It depends what you count.** Measured with total compensation and national income, the US labour share barely moved between 1970 (66%) and 2006 (64%) <Cite id="feldstein2008" at="abstract" />. Our own BEA chart agrees that today's compensation share is near its 1929 level.
- **Outside the US it may not be falling at all.** Once housing and self-employment are treated consistently, labour shares are stable or rising in major advanced economies other than the US and Canada, which casts doubt on most technological explanations <Cite id="gutierrez2020" at="abstract" />.
- **Market power, not machines.** In the US, most of the decline comes from sales shifting to "superstar" firms with low labour shares, not from falling shares inside firms <Cite id="autor2020superstar" at="abstract" />.
</Skeptics>
