<Aside type="tip" title="TL;DR">
If workers matter less to production, their slice of income should shrink. In the US it has: the official labour share of business income is lower now than at any time before 2000. But the long-run picture is a hump, not a cliff, and part of the story is benefits replacing wages.
</Aside>

<Tag kind="evidence" />The Bureau of Labor Statistics tracks how much of nonfarm business output is paid to workers, including an estimate for the self-employed. From 1947 to 2000 the index never fell below {f1(pre2000Low)}. In {last(ls).x} it averaged {f1(last(ls).v)} (2017 = 100).

## Wages vs total compensation

<Tag kind="evidence" />A wider lens, the Bureau of Economic Analysis's share of all US income, tells a subtler story. Total compensation peaked at {f1(peak(comp).v)}% of income in {peak(comp).x} and stood at {f1(last(comp).v)}% in {last(comp).x}, close to where it was in 1929 ({f1(comp[0].v)}%). Wages and salaries alone fell further, from {f1(wages[0].v)}% in {wages[0].x} to {f1(last(wages).v)}%, because a growing slice of pay now arrives as benefits such as health insurance and pensions.

## Not just an American story

<Tag kind="evidence" />Across countries, the labour share has declined significantly since the early 1980s; the authors attribute roughly half of the fall to the falling relative price of investment goods, which made it cheaper to replace labour with capital <Cite id="karabarbounis2014" at="abstract" />.

## Why it matters for post-labor

Post-Labor Economics reads this decline as the early phase of the Great Decoupling: output keeps growing while the share paid for human work shrinks <Cite id="shapiro2025six" />. If that continues, more income flows to whoever owns the machines, which is why PLE focuses on ownership rather than wages <Cite id="piketty2014" />.

<Aside type="caution" title="What skeptics say">
Measured with total compensation and national income, the US labour share barely moved between 1970 (66%) and 2006 (64%) <Cite id="feldstein2008" at="abstract" />. Our own BEA chart agrees that today's compensation share is near its 1929 level. The fall is real in the business-sector series, but its size depends on what you count as labour income and over which years.
</Aside>
