<Aside type="tip" title="TL;DR">
Since 1973, output per hour in US business has grown much faster than the average worker's real hourly pay. Everyone agrees the lines diverge; they disagree on how much, depending on which worker, which pay and which price index you use.
</Aside>

## Three ways to measure the same gap

1. **Which worker.** The chart shows the *average*. If pay at the top grows fastest, the typical worker's pay grows less. The Economic Policy Institute tracks typical workers and finds a much wider gap <Cite id="bivens2015" />.
2. **Which pay.** Wages alone exclude health insurance and pensions, which have taken a growing share of compensation (see [labour's share of income](/evidence/labour-share/)).
3. **Which prices.** Productivity is measured in the prices of what workers make; pay is usually deflated by the prices of what workers buy. Use the same index for both and much of the gap disappears <Cite id="feldstein2008" at="abstract" />.

## Why it matters for post-labor

If gains from productivity stop reaching workers, growth alone will not carry household incomes. That is the core empirical premise of the Great Decoupling <Cite id="shapiro2026laborzero" />.

<Aside type="caution" title="What skeptics say">
The link is weakened, not broken: from 1973 to 2016, each extra point of productivity growth went with 0.7 to 1 point more growth in median and average pay, and 0.4 to 0.7 points for production workers <Cite id="stansbury2017" at="abstract" />. On this reading, other forces such as weaker bargaining power suppressed pay, rather than productivity escaping workers entirely.
</Aside>
