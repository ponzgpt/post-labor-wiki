<Aside type="tip" title="TL;DR">
Headline numbers about automation measure very different things: exposure, risk, local effects or productivity. Read the method before the number. The evidence so far shows real displacement in specific places and tasks, and no economy-wide collapse in work, yet.
</Aside>

## Exposure is not job loss

<Tag kind="forecast" />In 2013, an occupation-level model put about 47% of US employment at high risk of computerisation <Cite id="frey2017" />. A task-level method applied to 21 OECD countries found about 9% <Cite id="arntz2016" at="abstract" />. The difference: whole occupations rarely automate, because most jobs mix easy and hard tasks.

<Tag kind="evidence" />For large language models, about 80% of US workers have at least 10% of their tasks exposed, and about 19% have at least half; the authors make no prediction about timing <Cite id="eloundou2024" at="abstract" />.

## What has actually been measured

| Study | What it measured | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| <Cite id="autor2003" /> | Which tasks computers took over, 1960–1998 | Computers replace routine tasks and complement problem-solving work |
| <Cite id="acemoglu2017wp" /> | Industrial robots in US local labour markets, 1990–2007 | One more robot per 1,000 workers: employment-to-population down 0.18–0.34 points, wages down 0.25–0.5% (abstract) |
| <Cite id="acemoglu2019" /> | Automation vs new tasks across the economy | Displacement has outpaced the creation of new tasks in recent decades |
| <Cite id="brynjolfsson2025" /> | An AI assistant for 5,172 support agents | Productivity up 15% on average, mostly for less experienced agents (abstract) |
| <Cite id="acemoglu2024" /> | AI's economy-wide effect, task-based model | No more than 0.66% extra productivity over ten years (abstract) |

## Why it matters for post-labor

Post-Labor Economics argues that the old pattern, where displaced workers move to new tasks, breaks once machines are better, faster, cheaper and safer at most of them, including cognitive work <Cite id="shapiro2025six" />. The question becomes empirical: are new tasks still appearing faster than old ones disappear?

<Aside type="caution" title="What skeptics say">
Two centuries of predictions that machines would end work have been wrong so far <Cite id="mokyr2015" />, and weak job growth in rich countries may reflect slow economic growth more than fast automation <Cite id="benanav2020" />.
</Aside>
