<Aside type="tip" title="In short">
Read the method before the number. Exposure measures what AI could touch, not what it will replace or when. Measured effects so far are small overall, with the first signs concentrated among young workers in the most exposed occupations.
</Aside>

## From occupations to tasks

<Tag kind="empirical" />Computers replace routine tasks and complement problem-solving work <Cite id="autor2003" at="abstract" />. This task view changed the numbers: an occupation-level model put about 47% of US employment at high risk of computerisation <Cite id="frey2017" at="abstract" />; a task-level method for 21 OECD countries found about 9% <Cite id="arntz2016" at="abstract" />. Most jobs mix tasks machines can do with tasks they cannot.

<Tag kind="analytical" />The framework behind most current work: automation displaces labour from tasks, new tasks reinstate it, and in recent decades displacement has outpaced reinstatement <Cite id="acemoglu2019" at="abstract" />.

## AI exposure indices

<Tag kind="forecast" /><Term id="automation-exposure">Exposure indices</Term> score occupations by how much of their task content AI could perform. For large language models, about 80% of US workers have at least 10% of their tasks exposed, and about 19% at least half; the authors make no prediction about timing <Cite id="eloundou2024" at="abstract" />. Earlier, Felten, Raj and Seamans built an occupation, industry and county index of AI exposure <Cite id="felten2021" at="abstract" />. The ILO's global index finds exposure rising with income: 11% of employment in low-income countries, 34% in high-income ones <Cite id="gmyrek2025" at="abstract" />.

## What has actually been measured

| Study | Setting | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| <Cite id="acemoglu2017wp" at="abstract" /> | Industrial robots, US local labour markets, 1990–2007 | One more robot per 1,000 workers: employment-to-population down 0.18–0.34 points, wages down 0.25–0.5% |
| <Cite id="brynjolfsson2025" at="abstract" /> | AI assistant, 5,172 support agents | Productivity up 15% on average, mostly for less experienced agents |
| <Cite id="humlum2025" at="abstract" /> | Danish workers and workplaces, two years after ChatGPT | Precise null effects on earnings and hours; effects above 2% ruled out |
| <Cite id="brynjolfsson2025canaries" at="abstract" /> | US payroll records to mid-2026 | Workers aged 22–25 in exposed occupations about 19% below their counterfactual; no economy-wide displacement |
| <Cite id="acemoglu2024" at="abstract" /> | Task-based macro model | <Tag kind="forecast" />No more than 0.66% extra total factor productivity over ten years |

The young-worker gap works mainly through less hiring, and concentrates where AI substitutes for tasks rather than complementing them; the authors call the evidence descriptive, not causal <Cite id="brynjolfsson2025canaries" at="abstract" />.

## Why it matters for post-labor

<Tag kind="analytical" />Post-Labor Economics argues that the old escape route, moving to new tasks, closes once machines are better, faster, cheaper and safer at most of them, cognitive work included <Cite id="shapiro2026seven" at="intuition 1" />. That makes the next few years testable: watch whether the entry-level gap spreads to older workers and to the aggregate.

<Skeptics>
- **New work keeps arriving.** Most current US employment is in job specialties introduced since 1940, though the job-eroding effect of automation innovations has intensified since 1980 <Cite id="autor2024newwork" at="abstract" />.
- **Weak job growth has other causes.** Slow economic growth, not fast automation, may explain weak labour demand in rich countries <Cite id="benanav2020" at="chs. 3–4" />.
- **Alarm is old.** Anxiety that machines will end work has recurred since the late 18th century <Cite id="mokyr2015" at="abstract" />; so far automation has also complemented labour and raised demand for it <Cite id="autor2015" at="abstract" />.
</Skeptics>
