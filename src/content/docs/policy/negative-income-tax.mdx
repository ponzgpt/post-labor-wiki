## The design

<Tag kind="normative" />A negative income tax (NIT) pays a guaranteed amount to someone with no earnings and withdraws it gradually, at a set rate, as earnings rise, so working always leaves people better off. Milton Friedman proposed it in 1962 as a replacement for the patchwork of welfare programmes <Cite id="friedman1962" at="ch. XII" />. In principle a UBI taxed back at the same rate gives the same net income at every level of earnings: the two differ mainly in who receives a cheque, and when.

## The experiments

<Tag kind="empirical" />The US ran four randomised NIT experiments between 1968 and 1982. Their consensus result: work fell, modestly. On average husbands reduced work by about two weeks of full-time employment a year, wives and single mothers by about three weeks, and young people by about four <Cite id="robins1985" at="abstract" />. Canada's Mincome, a guaranteed annual income in Manitoba, was later linked to 8.5% fewer hospitalisations <Cite id="forget2011" at="abstract" />.

## What was enacted instead

<Tag kind="empirical" />The US chose the Earned Income Tax Credit, which pays only those who work. Its labour supply effects are positive for single mothers and its impact on poverty rates is large; some of the benefit is captured by employers through lower wages <Cite id="nichols2015" at="abstract" />.

## Why it matters for post-labor

<Tag kind="analytical" />An NIT is cheaper than a UBI on paper because it pays only those below the break-even point. But it still keys support to earnings, and its withdrawal rate acts like a tax on extra work. In an economy with little paid work, the difference between an NIT and a UBI shrinks: almost everyone would be below the break-even line.

<Skeptics>
- **High implicit tax rates.** Withdrawing benefits as earnings rise acts like a marginal tax; the experiments show people did respond by working somewhat less <Cite id="robins1985" at="abstract" />.
- **Work-tested credits do more for employment.** Where the policy goal is more work, the EITC's record is stronger than an unconditional floor's <Cite id="nichols2015" at="abstract" />.
- **The politics went the other way.** Despite the experiments, no country adopted a full NIT; the US built a work-conditioned credit instead.
</Skeptics>
