## What it is

Post-Labor Economics (PLE) is David Shapiro's framework for an economy in which machines do most production. It asks how households get an income, and citizens keep power, once wages stop being the main channel <Cite id="shapiro2025six" at="introduction" />. He frames ending obligatory work as a design problem whose progress can be measured <Cite id="shapiro2025movement" at="launch post" />.

His own syllabus has six steps: the rise of automation, the decline of labour, power and social contracts, measurement, concrete interventions, and life after labour <Cite id="shapiro2025six" at="parts I–VI" />. The book <cite>Labor Zero</cite> (2026) develops the same arc; its free [online companion](https://daveshap.github.io/PostLaborEconomics/laborzero/) carries the plates, an appendix and the bibliography <Cite id="shapiro2026laborzero" at="companion site" />.

## The moves that make it distinctive

- **Laborism as the obstacle.** Shapiro argues that left and right share a belief that work must be preserved, and that this blocks honest planning <Cite id="shapiro2025movement" at="launch post" />.
- **Measurement first.** He proposes indices that split household income into wages, property returns and transfers, so the shift can be tracked <Cite id="shapiro2025six" at="part IV" />. See [economic agency](/frameworks/economic-agency/).
- **Power, not just money.** Without new leverage, he expects the default to be technofeudalism, not abundance <Cite id="shapiro2026technofeudalism" at="parts II–IV" />.
- **Path dependence.** The same technology can lead to a cyberpunk or a solarpunk future; policy picks the attractor <Cite id="shapiro2025six" at="part VI" />.

<Skeptics>
- **The premise is not yet visible in jobs.** Prime-age employment is near record highs, and the most careful early studies of generative AI find small or null effects on earnings and hours <Cite id="humlum2025" at="abstract" />.
- **New work keeps appearing.** Most US employment today is in job specialties introduced since 1940 <Cite id="autor2024newwork" at="abstract" />. The burden is on PLE to show why this mechanism stops.
- **The labour-share decline may not be about machines.** Outside the US and Canada, harmonised labour shares are stable or rising, which casts doubt on purely technological explanations <Cite id="gutierrez2020" at="abstract" />.
- **Timelines are forecasts.** The 2040–2060 window rests on capability and adoption assumptions that economists' task-based models do not share <Cite id="acemoglu2024" at="abstract" />.

Shapiro's reply is that these readings look backwards, while the shift he describes is driven by AI agents and robots now arriving <Cite id="shapiro2025six" at="part I" />.
</Skeptics>
